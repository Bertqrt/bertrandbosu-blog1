When I was about six, my mom and I were standing in a line, waiting for something. I don't even remember what. There was a tree nearby dropping its leaves, and I asked her: *"Why do the green leaves fall too? Shouldn't it be only the brown ones?"*

I don't remember asking it either. My mom is the one who tells the story. Apparently a man standing next to us in the line heard me and said it was a very deep question. I'm pretty sure I just meant the leaves.

But that question has been sitting in my head lately, and I think six-year-old me was onto something. Just not about trees.

## The tree has a reason

Green leaves really do fall, and sometimes the tree drops them on purpose. In a drought, every leaf is losing water into the air, and the tree can't afford to keep all of them. So it lets some go, healthy ones included, just to <a href="https://eos.org/editor-highlights/trees-shed-their-leaves-to-adapt-to-droughts" target="_blank" rel="noopener">survive</a>.

So the tree isn't being stupid. It's making a hard call with the little water it has.

We do the same thing with ideas. Except most of the time, we don't have a reason.

## We cut the green ones

Think about how many ideas die before they even get a chance. They're still budding, still growing, and someone shoots them down. Sometimes that someone is us.

**I think we kill our own ideas out of fear, and other people's out of doubt.**

I already wrote about the first one in [The fear of starting]({{ site.baseurl }}{% post_url 2026-09-29-the-fear-of-starting %}). You want to start something, a voice says *what if you mess it up?*, and the idea dies right there in your head.

The second one is sneakier. Someone tells you their plan, and before they've even finished you're listing why it won't work. *That's been done before. Where will you get the money? Be serious.* It feels like being realistic. Most of the time, the idea is just new and we don't know what to do with it.

I've done it to my own ideas plenty. A life-sim game set in Ghana, something like Lagos Life. Properly learning full-stack web dev, frontend and backend. Both are still sitting in my head, green and untouched. The one I actually let grow was a `/study` command for my Telegram bot that pulls answers from my own study material. It's only a prototype, but it exists, and it only exists because I didn't cut it early.

## Matthea's side of it

I talked all of this through with my friend Matthea, and she wrote something about the green leaves that I couldn't say better myself. Here it is in her words:

> Green leaves fall too.
>
> Ideas fresh enough, but not given the grace to grow, because they take up the space we call secure. They demand time, and resources that could have nourished other prospects, other tangible things that have proved more promising and stable.
>
> They pose as risks  because they don't have track records for being the most promising.
>
> They don't have the mature wear of the many tried and true others.
>
> And it's all true, no argument there.
>
> It's also true that we could be wrong.
>
> The new might just be the much-awaited solutions for the problems of old. New things bring the constant change life needs, the variety experience craves, the shift perspective requires; they are simply what makes the world go round.
>
> And the thrill that comes with new ventures couldn't be more in common with  metaphorical energy the sun's rays give to trees.
>
> At the very least they are proof that we still have curious, innovative brains.
>
> Do we really want to look back and see what-could-have-beens, what-was-theres? Do we really?
>
> For the best part green leaves are what make trees the colour we know trees for.
>
> Health, hope, home.
{: .guest-piece}

What I like about how she put it is that she doesn't pretend the doubt is stupid. *"And it's all true, no argument there."* New ideas really do eat time and resources, and they really don't have a track record. She just adds the line most of us skip: *it's also true that we could be wrong.*

That one stuck with me. My `/study` command had no track record either. Neither does the Ghana game. The only difference between them is that one of them got a chance.

## And we keep the brown ones

And while we're busy cutting the green leaves, we're holding on to the brown ones for dear life.

Matthea calls them *the many tried and true others*, and some of them really are tried and true. But some are just old. Old habits. Old ways of thinking. Patterns we can clearly see aren't good for us, but we still give them room.

Mine is sticking to what I already know. Almost everything I build runs on an Arduino or an ESP32: a smart waste bin, an RFID access lock with an MFRC522. The moment something needs a Raspberry Pi, like the OCR reading wand I want to make, I quietly push it aside, because I don't know how to use one. Arduinos aren't bad. They're just familiar, and that's exactly why I keep reaching for them.

To a six-year-old, that's as backwards as it gets. Drop the healthy leaves and keep the dying ones? Chale, even the tree knows better.

## It's the same thing underneath

Fear and doubt feel like two different things, but I don't think they are. Underneath both, **we just don't like not knowing.**

A new idea, yours or someone else's, comes with no guarantee. You can't tell if it'll work. The old habit, as bad as it is, is familiar. You already know what it costs you. So we pick the familiar thing, even when it's dying. Matthea's phrase for it is *the space we call secure*. A new idea threatens that space, so we cut it before it can take any.

A <a href="https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/is-there-a-hidden-bias-against-creativity.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">2012 study</a> found something close to this. People said they valued creative ideas, but when they felt uncertain, they quietly preferred the practical, familiar ones. The uncertainty even made them worse at recognizing a creative idea when they saw one.

## Noticing is most of it

A six-year-old saw this from one tree while standing in a line, so it's not exactly hidden. We just stop looking.

What I'm trying now is small. When a friend tells me an idea, I ask one question about how it could work before I say anything about why it won't. When an idea of my own shows up, I write it down instead of killing it in my head that same night. Most of them won't survive anyway, and that's fine. At least they'll fall after a fair chance, instead of ending up as one of Matthea's *what-could-have-beens*.

The brown leaf is harder. I haven't dropped mine yet. But I've stopped pretending it's still green, and I think that's where letting go starts.

> What's one green leaf when there are millions more?<br>It's still one green leaf either way.
>
