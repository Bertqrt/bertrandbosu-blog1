Just like a drug, the initial high (you could even call it ecstasy) of a new goal wears off with time. One really motivating speech gives you this strong hope, this drive to keep going after something you know is within reach. Then the next day it just doesn't hit the same. Kind of like developing tolerance to that drug. Yesterday's inspiring quote doesn't move you today, so you figure you need a stronger dose of inspiration to get back to that same focus.

I noticed this happening to me for weeks, and I kept thinking to myself: is this actually how motivation is supposed to work, or am I just broken somehow. It bugged me enough that I decided to look into it more.

Turns out chasing that stronger dose is exactly the wrong move.

There's a real name for what's happening: [reward prediction error](https://www.brainfacts.org/brain-anatomy-and-function/genes-and-molecules/2021/discovering-dopamines-role-in-reward-prediction-error-122121). Dopamine doesn't fire because something is good, it fires because something is unexpected. The first time a quote hits, your brain didn't see it coming, so it lands hard. Once it learns to expect that kind of hit, it stops responding, even though nothing about the quote changed. [One researcher](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/science-of-choice/202001/how-do-we-learn-from-our-mistakes) even describes this using the exact word you'd use for a drug: tolerance.

So chasing a bigger dose doesn't fix anything. It just trains you to need an even bigger one next time. Same trap, steeper slope.

What actually holds up is motivation you're not borrowing from someone else's speech. [Self-determination theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-determination_theory) points at three things instead: you're doing it because you chose to, you can feel yourself getting better at it, and it means something beyond just you. That kind of drive doesn't spike and crash, because it was never a hit to begin with.

Motivation gets you to start. It was never going to be the thing that keeps you going. That's why I think motivation kinda sucks. It's real, it's just not built to last, and expecting it to is what keeps letting me down.
