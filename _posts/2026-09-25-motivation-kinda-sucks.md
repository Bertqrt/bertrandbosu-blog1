You're probably thinking, "what is this guy even talking about??" Honestly, I wasn't too sure myself. I went and did some digging, and I'll be upfront, I didn't come out the other side with some clean, definite answer. But I did find enough to actually explain why this keeps happening to me, and maybe why it's happening to you too.

Just like a drug, the initial high (you could even call it ecstasy) of a new goal wears off with time. One really motivating speech gives you this strong hope, this drive to keep going after something you know is within reach. Then the next day it just doesn't hit the same. Kind of like developing tolerance to that drug. Yesterday's inspiring quote doesn't move you today, so you figure you need a stronger dose of inspiration to get back to that same focus, and the cycle just keeps repeating itself.

I noticed this happening to me for weeks, and I kept asking myself: *is this actually how motivation is supposed to work, or am I just broken somehow?* It bugged me enough that I decided to look into it more.

Turns out chasing that stronger dose is exactly the wrong move.

There's a real name for what's happening: **<a href="https://www.brainfacts.org/brain-anatomy-and-function/genes-and-molecules/2021/discovering-dopamines-role-in-reward-prediction-error-122121" target="_blank" rel="noopener">reward prediction error</a>**. The neuroscientist who mapped this out, **<a href="https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/science-of-choice/202001/how-do-we-learn-from-our-mistakes" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Wolfram Schultz</a>**, found that dopamine doesn't fire because something is *good*, it fires because something is *unexpected*. The first time a quote hits, your brain didn't see it coming, so it lands hard. Once it learns to expect that kind of hit, it stops responding, even though nothing about the quote itself changed. Schultz even describes this using the exact word you'd use for a drug: *tolerance*.

So chasing a bigger dose doesn't fix anything. It just trains you to need an even bigger one next time. Same trap, steeper slope.

What actually holds up is motivation you're not borrowing from someone else's speech. **<a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-determination_theory" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Self-determination theory</a>**, put together by psychologists **Edward Deci** and **Richard Ryan**, points at three things instead: you're doing it because you chose to (autonomy), you can feel yourself getting better at it (competence), and it means something beyond just you (relatedness). That kind of drive doesn't spike and crash, because it was never a hit to begin with.

Motivation gets you to start. It was never going to be the thing that keeps you going. That's why I think motivation kinda sucks. It's real, it's just not built to last, and expecting it to is what keeps letting me down. I still don't have a clean fix for this, not for me anyway. Some problems don't really come with a real answer, at least not one you can act on right away. Sometimes all you get is knowing what's actually going on, so you stop blaming yourself for it. I'll take that over another hit of inspiration.
